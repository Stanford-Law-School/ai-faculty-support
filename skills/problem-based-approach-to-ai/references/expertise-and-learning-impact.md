# Expertise and learning impact

In a law school, how a tool affects skill and learning is a first-class consideration, not an afterthought. This is the E in PAUSE.

## Ask

Does the proposed use:

- Strengthen learning?
- Support judgment?
- Make reasoning visible?
- Build verification habits?
- Expand responsible capacity?

Or does it instead:

- Encourage dependency?
- Conceal reasoning?
- Remove necessary practice?
- Weaken expertise over time?
- Substitute automation for accountable judgment?

## Students

- Does the use preserve the practice students need to develop (reading cases, constructing arguments, checking authority)?
- Does it make the reasoning visible and checkable, or hand over a finished answer?
- Is there a genuine non-AI path so no student is forced into a tool?
- Would routine use erode a skill the course is meant to build?

## Faculty and staff

- Does the use keep the expert in command of the judgment, or gradually deskill them?
- Does it build verification habits, or train people to trust output uncritically?
- Would losing the tool leave the person less capable than before?

## Reading the result

A use that saves time but quietly removes essential practice or hides reasoning can be a poor choice even when efficient. Say so, and suggest designs that keep learning and judgment intact (for example, AI that critiques a student's draft rather than producing it).
