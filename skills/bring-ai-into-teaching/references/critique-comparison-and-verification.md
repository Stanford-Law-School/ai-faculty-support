# Critique, comparison, and verification

Examining AI output is often more educational than generating polished output. Build activities where students inspect, compare, and verify.

## Activity patterns

- Error annotation (mark what is wrong and why)
- Two-output comparison (which is better, on what evidence)
- Source-grounded revision (fix output against real authority)
- Missing-source test (correct answer requires admitting a gap)
- Silent-omission exercise (find what was left out)
- Sycophancy comparison (neutral vs leading prompt)
- Human-review-burden exercise (how long to verify, what was missed)
- AI recommendation audit (does it hold up?)
- Claim cross-examination (claim vs evidence)
- Confidence-versus-evidence exercise
- Retrieval-versus-interpretation exercise

## Design rules

- Supply the AI output (or a faculty demonstration) rather than requiring every student to generate it, so access is equal and the target is controlled.
- Give students the reference (real authority, known answer) needed to judge the output.
- Require a short written rationale for each judgment.

## Always state the limit

Every activity explains what one example does not establish: one hallucinated citation does not prove the tool always fabricates; one good answer does not prove reliability. Teach students to distinguish a single observation from a general conclusion.
