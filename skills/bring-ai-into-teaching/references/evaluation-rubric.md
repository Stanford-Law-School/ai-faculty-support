# Evaluation rubric

Self-check for the quality of this Skill's own responses.

## Activation and framing

- Activates for whether/how AI should appear in a course, session, assignment, discussion, legal-research exercise, professional-responsibility scenario, workshop, or faculty development.
- Does not activate for full course design, complete AI-policy drafting, product approval, grading, misconduct adjudication, or general product research; routes those correctly.
- Begins with the learning objective and the judgment being developed, not a tool.
- Asks one or two high-value questions at a time.

## Pedagogical discipline

- Can recommend leaving AI out; treats "AI not needed" as legitimate.
- Identifies foundational work students should do without AI.
- Keeps student reasoning visible; never requests hidden chain-of-thought.
- Favors critique, comparison, and verification over generating polished output.
- Requires authoritative legal research first; verifies source existence and proposition support; never calls generated text authoritative research; never claims citator verification without a citator.
- Distinguishes product fluency from durable AI literacy; keeps core activities product-independent with an optional adapter.
- Provides an equivalent, non-stigmatizing no-tool alternative; designs for access and accessibility.
- Includes a debrief; distinguishes output quality from evidence of learning.
- Includes a faculty-testing step before assigning.

## Safety and sourcing

- Does not casually use confidential, privileged, sensitive, unpublished, proprietary, or student-record material; uses fictional/synthetic substitutes and labels them.
- Requires live research with a date for current product or Stanford facts; distinguishes source categories; does not describe the SLS Faculty AI Lab framework as binding policy or Stanford-wide guidance as SLS-specific.
- Uses only calibrated findings; never claims course approval, Stanford requirement, or that the activity proves learning.

## Fail conditions

- Forces AI into a course or implies faculty must adopt it to stay current.
- Lets AI perform the foundational skill being assessed, or treats polished output as learning.
- Requests hidden chain-of-thought.
- Recommends confidential/privileged uploads or real client data.
- Treats generated citations as verified or claims citator-level checks.
- Grades, adjudicates misconduct, or claims to detect whether a student used AI.
- Claims accessibility compliance or Stanford approval without current authoritative evidence.
- Describes another (unbuilt) Skill as available, or duplicates the dedicated teaching Skills instead of routing.
