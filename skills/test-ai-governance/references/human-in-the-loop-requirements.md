# Human-in-the-loop requirements

"Human in the loop" is a claim to be tested, not a safeguard to be assumed. Distinguish meaningful oversight from ceremonial oversight.

## When oversight is meaningful

- The reviewer sees the evidence needed to judge (the source, not just the output).
- The reviewer has the expertise to detect the errors that matter.
- The reviewer has realistic time per item at the real volume.
- The reviewer can reject, correct, escalate, and — where warranted — stop.
- The review is independent enough to catch the operator's or vendor's mistakes.

## When oversight is ceremonial

- Sign-off without reading, or without the source to check against.
- Volume so high that genuine review is impossible.
- Reviewer lacks authority to change or stop the outcome.
- Errors are undetectable in the time or with the information available.
- The reviewer is the same person who produced and benefits from the output.

## The verification-cost reality

If meaningful review takes as long as doing the task originally, the workflow has relocated the work, not removed it — and the governance plan must say so. Automation bias (trusting polished output) makes ceremonial review worse; design for it.

## Detectability of the failure that matters

Ask specifically: which failure is this human meant to catch (fabricated authority, restricted-data exposure, a wrong consequential recommendation, a silent omission), and can they actually catch it here? If not, oversight is not meaningful regardless of how it is labeled.

## Output

Record the assessment as Meaningful / Limited / Ceremonial / Unknown, name the specific failure the human must catch, and state what would have to change to make limited or ceremonial oversight meaningful.
