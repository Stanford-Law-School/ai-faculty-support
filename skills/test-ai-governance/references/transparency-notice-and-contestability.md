# Transparency, notice, and contestability

People affected by an AI-influenced outcome should be able to understand that AI was involved and to challenge the result. Notice is necessary but not sufficient — it is not authorization or consent.

## What users and affected people may need to know

- That AI is used
- What role it plays
- What it does not decide
- What data it uses
- Whether a human reviews it
- How to report an error
- How to request correction
- Whether an alternative exists
- What records are retained

## Notice is not consent

- Telling people AI is used does not authorize the use.
- A checkbox does not settle appropriateness for consequential or non-optional contexts.
- Transparency reduces surprise; it does not transfer accountability to the affected person.

## Contestability

For consequential or public-facing use, people should have a genuine path to question an outcome and reach a human who can act. Contestability is hollow if the only reviewer is the system or a person without authority to change the result.

## Drafting notices

If asked to draft a notice, clearly label it a draft for review and do not present it as an official Stanford or SLS notice. Institutional wording requires authorized review.

## Boundary

Design transparency and contestability as governance requirements; do not promise a specific Stanford procedure unless current official guidance establishes it.
